# Observability & Site Reliability Engineering (SRE)

> **Domain**: System Telemetry, Performance Metrics, Distributed Profiling, SRE Reliability Frameworks  
> **Index Numbers**: 58, 59, 60, 61, 62, 63, 64, 65, 66, 91, 92, 93, 94, 109, 110, 111

## 58. Monitoring

### What
The continuous, automated collection, aggregation, and analysis of predetermined system health signals, states, and error outputs to verify whether components are functioning within expected operational parameters.

### Why
- Provides operational visibility into infrastructure and application health.
- Alerts engineering teams when predefined thresholds are breached, enabling rapid incident intervention.

### Where We Use Them
- Server monitoring (CPU, RAM, disk, network utilization).
- Uptime checkers (Pingdom, Datadog Synthetics), container dashboards, cloud provider monitoring (AWS CloudWatch, GCP Cloud Monitoring).

---

## 59. Logging

### What
The structured or unstructured recording of significant events, errors, operational steps, and state transitions generated by application code and system components, timestamped and stored for inspection.

### Why
- Provides an immutable audit trail and historical record essential for forensic root-cause analysis after an incident or bug occurs.
- Reveals the contextual details (stack traces, user IDs, request inputs) of unexpected exceptions.

### Where We Use Them
- Structured logging (JSON format with level, timestamp, correlation ID, service name).
- Log aggregation pipelines: Elastic Stack (Elasticsearch, Logstash, Kibana - ELK), Grafana Loki, AWS CloudWatch Logs, Splunk.

---

## 60. Distributed Tracing

### What
A method for profiling and tracking the end-to-end execution path of a single user request as it traverses across dozens of decoupled microservices, message queues, and databases in a distributed architecture.

### Why
- In a microservices architecture, a single frontend click may trigger 20 downstream RPC calls; traditional single-server logs cannot identify which specific service or query introduced a 2-second bottleneck.
- Correlates all spans using a shared `TraceID` and parent `SpanID`.

### Where We Use Them
- OpenTelemetry (OTel) standards, Jaeger, Zipkin, AWS X-Ray, Datadog APM.
- Diagnosing distributed latency bottlenecks, pinpointing failing downstream microservices.

---

## 61. Metrics

### What
Numeric measurements and aggregations captured at regular intervals over time (time-series data) representing system performance, resource consumption, or business velocity. Typically categorized into Counters, Gauges, and Histograms.

### Why
- Extremely lightweight to store, transmit, and query compared to verbose raw text logs.
- Powers real-time visual dashboards, mathematical trend forecasting, and automated autoscaling triggers.

### Where We Use Them
- The "Four Golden Signals" (Latency, Traffic, Errors, Saturation).
- Technologies: Prometheus, InfluxDB, Grafana, Datadog.

---

## 62. Alerts

### What
Automated notifications dispatched to on-call engineering teams or incident channels (Slack, PagerDuty, email, SMS) when critical operational metrics breach acceptable boundaries or when system anomalies are detected.

### Why
- Ensures immediate human or automated intervention during customer-impacting outages without requiring engineers to stare at dashboards 24/7.
- Minimizes Mean Time to Detect (MTTD) and Mean Time to Acknowledge (MTTA).

### Where We Use Them
- Alertmanager (Prometheus), PagerDuty, Opsgenie, Datadog Monitors.
- High error rate alarms (e.g., HTTP 5xx errors > 1% over 5 minutes), disk space < 15%, synthetic probe failures.

---

## 63. SLOs (Service Level Objectives)

### What
A target metric or range of values agreed upon by engineering and business teams that specifies the expected reliability level of a service (e.g., "99.9% of successful HTTP requests must return in under 200ms over a rolling 30-day window").

### Why
- Serves as the internal engineering target to balance feature velocity against system stability.
- Aligns engineering priorities: if the SLO is healthy, ship features fast; if the SLO is breached, focus exclusively on reliability.

### Where We Use Them
- Defined in SRE frameworks for microservices, public APIs, database latency targets, and payment pipelines.

---

## 64. SLIs (Service Level Indicators)

### What
A quantifiable, direct metric that measures the compliance of a service with a target SLO at any given point in time. Typically calculated as:
$$\text{SLI} = \frac{\text{Good Events}}{\text{Total Valid Events}} \times 100\%$$

### Why
- Provides the objective mathematical ground truth required to evaluate whether an SLO is met or missed.
- Removes subjective opinions ("the app feels slow today") in favor of concrete data.

### Where We Use Them
- Ratio of HTTP status < 500 to total HTTP requests; ratio of read requests completing under 100ms to total read requests.

---

## 65. Error Budgets

### What
The inverse of an SLO ($100\% - \text{SLO}$), representing the maximum allowable amount of unreliability, downtime, or failed transactions a service can incur over a specified time period (e.g., a 99.9% SLO allows a 0.1% error budget, or ~43 minutes of downtime per month).

### Why
- Eliminates the myth of "100% uptime" (which is impossibly expensive and stops innovation).
- Transforms reliability into a quantifiable currency: engineering teams can spend this budget to take risks, deploy fast, and run chaos experiments. If the budget is exhausted, releases freeze until stability is restored.

### Where We Use Them
- SRE governance meetings, automated deployment gate checks in CI/CD pipelines (blocking deployments if the monthly error budget is burned).

---

## 66. Observability

### What
A measure of how well the internal state of a software system can be inferred solely from knowledge of its external outputs (telemetry data). In modern software engineering, it is realized through the unification of the **Three Pillars of Observability**: Logs, Metrics, and Traces (M.E.L.T.).

### Why
- Traditional monitoring tells you *when* a known failure occurs ("Service X is down"); Observability enables you to understand *why* novel, unforeseen, and complex failures occur in distributed systems without deploying new code.

### Where We Use Them
- OpenTelemetry instrumented stacks, Honeycomb, Datadog, Dynatrace, New Relic.

---

## 91. Latency

### What
The duration of time it takes for a data packet, request, or transaction to travel from its point of origin to its destination, execute processing, and return the response back to the client.

### Why
- Directly impacts user conversion, user satisfaction, and business revenue (Amazon famously noted that every 100ms of added latency cost 1% in sales).
- Identifies network bottlenecks, serialization overhead, and slow disk I/O.

### Where We Use Them
- Measuring end-to-end API response time, database query roundtrip time, network roundtrip time (RTT ping).

---

## 92. Throughput

### What
The number of units of work, requests, operations, or data bytes a system, service, or network channel can successfully process and deliver within a given unit of time (e.g., Requests Per Second - RPS, Queries Per Second - QPS, or Megabytes Per Second - MB/s).

### Why
- Measures raw processing capacity and concurrency limits of infrastructure.
- Vital for capacity planning, load testing benchmarks, and determining when horizontal scaling is required.

### Where We Use Them
- Stress testing tools (k6, Locust, Apache JMeter), Kafka topic ingestion throughput, database write throughput benchmarks.

---

## 93. p99 Latency (99th Percentile Latency)

### What
A statistical metric indicating the latency threshold below which 99% of all monitored requests fall, meaning only 1% of the slowest requests took longer than this value.

### Why
- Average (mean) latency is dangerously misleading because a small number of fast requests hide extreme slow outliers experienced by users.
- In high-scale systems processing millions of requests, 1% of requests represents thousands of frustrating customer experiences.

### Where We Use Them
- SRE Service Level Agreements (SLAs), API gateway telemetry, e-commerce checkout latency monitoring.

---

## 94. Tail Latency

### What
The high-percentile latency (e.g., p95, p99, p99.9, p99.99) experienced by the unluckiest small fraction of requests at the long "tail" of the probability distribution curve.

### Why
- In distributed microservice systems, a single user request often fans out to dozens of backend services in parallel. If any one of those downstream calls experiences tail latency, the entire parent request suffers (the slowest sub-call dominates the response time).

### Where We Use Them
- Tackled via: Hedged requests (speculative parallel retries), request deadlines/timeouts, tuning Garbage Collection pauses, and optimizing thread pool saturation.

---

## 109. Production Incidents

### What
An unplanned event, outage, or significant service degradation that disrupts normal production operations, impairs system functionality, or violates user SLAs (e.g., severe database latency spikes, data corruption, total service downtime). Typically categorized into severity levels:
- **SEV-1 / P1 (Critical)**: Catastrophic outage affecting core customer journeys with no immediate workaround (all-hands bridge opened).
- **SEV-2 / P2 (Major)**: Significant degradation or major feature outage with partial workaround.
- **SEV-3 / P3 (Minor)**: Non-critical feature bug or minor performance dip.

### Why
- Establishes a rigorous, calm, and structured command structure (Incident Commander, Communications Lead, Operations Lead) during chaotic system failures.
- Minimizes Mean Time to Detect (MTTD), Mean Time to Acknowledge (MTTA), and Mean Time to Resolve (MTTR).

### Where We Use Them
- Incident management platforms: PagerDuty, Opsgenie, incident.io, FireHydrant.
- Automated War Room creation (dedicated Slack incident channels and Zoom bridges).

---

## 110. On-Call

### What
An operational engineering rotation where designated engineers, SREs, or developers are scheduled to be reachable 24/7 to receive automated alerts, respond to critical production incidents, and triage system emergencies outside standard working hours.

### Why
- Complex cloud systems and mission-critical applications run continuously; software defects, traffic spikes, or infrastructure failures can occur at any hour.
- Closes the feedback loop between development and operations: when engineers who build the software are on-call to maintain it, software reliability, test coverage, and documentation improve drastically.

### Where We Use Them
- Follow-the-sun on-call models (handing off alert duties across teams in US, EMEA, and APAC timezones).
- Automated escalation policies in PagerDuty or Opsgenie (escalating from Primary to Secondary engineer if unacknowledged within 5 minutes).

---

## 111. Postmortems (Blameless Postmortems)

### What
A structured, collaborative retrospective document and team review conducted after a major production incident resolves. It analyzes the timeline of events, root cause, contributing factors, financial/customer impact, and formulates prioritized action items to prevent the same failure mode from ever recurring.

### Why
- **Blameless Culture**: Operates under the premise that engineers do not deliberately make mistakes; failures are rooted in system architecture, missing safeguards, unclear tooling, or inadequate automated testing.
- Transforms costly production outages into permanent institutional resilience improvements.

### Where We Use Them
- Google SRE postmortem templates, Atlassian incident postmortems.
- Standard structure: Incident Summary, Timeline (UTC), Root Cause (Five Whys analysis), What Went Well, What Went Poorly, Where We Got Lucky, and Action Items (preventative Jira tickets assigned to owners).
